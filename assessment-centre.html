What happens on the day

Formats vary by firm. These are the exercises that come up most, and how people lose marks.

Group exercise

What it is
4–8 candidates discuss a business problem and agree a recommendation, often 20–40 minutes, assessors silent at the side.
Testing
Teamwork, influence, commercial judgement, structure.
How people fail
Saying nothing; talking over others; no numbers; no decision by the end.

Case study and presentation

What it is
A pack of company information, numbers and emails; you write a recommendation, then present it and take questions.
Testing
Analysis under time pressure, clear structure, defending a view.
How people fail
Summarising the pack instead of recommending; running out of time; no risks.

Inbox or written exercise

What it is
Emails and documents to prioritise and reply to, sometimes with an error to spot in the numbers.
Testing
Prioritising, attention to detail, professional writing, judgement.
How people fail
Doing the easy tasks first; missing the error; casual tone.

Interviews (superday)

What it is
Two to four back-to-back interviews, often analyst/associate, then VP, then MD or partner.
Testing
Motivation, your CV in depth, technicals, markets, and how you hold up by round three.
How people fail
Same story every round; vague “why us”; fading by the last interview.
What the assessor writes down

The group exercise scoring sheet

Assessors usually score a handful of behaviours on a 4-point scale and write down what you actually said. This is The Desk’s practice framework, not an employer’s internal scorecard. The AI group exercise scores you against it.

BehaviourWhat “strong” looks likeWhat gets marked down

Scale: 1 Below · 2 Developing · 3 Meets · 4 Strong. Assessors note evidence (“said X, brought in Y”), not impressions, so what you say out loud is what counts.

The night before

  • Print your crib sheet and read your six stories out loud once.
  • Read today’s business news: pick one story and your view on it.
  • Re-read the firm’s recent results or a deal it worked on.
  • Plan the journey (or test camera, sound and internet if virtual).
  • Prepare two questions for each interviewer.

On the day

  • In the group: speak in the first two minutes, then bring someone else in.
  • Use relevant numbers from the brief to support your comparisons.
  • Be the one who says “we’ve got five minutes, shall we agree?”
  • In the case study: recommendation first, then reasons, then risks.
  • Afterwards: tell us how it went, so the next student is better prepared.
